## Verified high-priority extraction

### Page 37 — CCF forestry operations

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006215849.jpg`. Harvest crews put marking decisions into effect while protecting economic and ecological values. The source contrasts relatively straightforward initiation in even-aged plantations with developed CCF stands' multiple sizes, irregular structure, diverse species, regeneration and biodiversity features. It focuses on mechanised harvester/forwarder work with some chainsaw assistance as current industry practice; smaller broadleaf thinning is often chainsaw based, and later-stage/developed CCF sometimes uses chainsaw felling of very large trees toward harvester reach. This is the book's account of practice, not a universal equipment requirement. Prior notes agree. Images: `P037-IMG-01–02`.

### Page 38 — Alternative extraction and timing

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006215916.jpg`. The book lists chainsaw felling with extraction alternatives including skidding, cable/skyline, modified agricultural machinery, tractor or quad with forestry trailer, mini forwarder and horse. It regards machine harvest as economic in most scenarios, while conservation, valuable timber, extreme gradients, owner environmental aims and financing may support other systems. Wet weather, contractor availability and licence conditions affect timing; foresters, owners and contractors should prioritise very sensitive sites and wetter stand areas during dry periods, while retaining year-round industry work. Prior notes are consistent but abbreviate the alternatives and scheduling constraints. Image: `P038-IMG-01`.

### Page 39 — Forest infrastructure and permanent racks

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006215922.jpg`. Compacted-stone forest roads, permanent headlands and tracks, and a permanent rack system divide a forest into compartments, stands and inter-rack areas. The book says this aids mapping, orientation, ground access for assessment and marking, and timber transport. It recommends confining machine travel to designated tracks and racks to protect soil, water, biodiversity and growing stock. Racks provide harvesting and extraction access, are permanent and as widely spaced as practicable; brash mats protect soil within racks and restriction of machine travel reduces compaction outside them.

For monitoring pure conifer stands before first thinning, purpose-cut **inspection paths**, not machine racks, are highly recommended after canopy closure. The box suggests **50–100 m apart** depending on stand/forest scale, with branches on both sides removed to head height using proper pruning cuts. This distance is **inspection-path spacing, not rack spacing**. The notes capture the distinction; the source additionally explains infrastructure's mapping and timber-transport roles. Image: `P039-IMG-01`.

### Page 40 — Rack spacing

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006215941.jpg`. The book says wider rack spacing in permanent CCF forests can assist stability and leave larger less-disturbed inter-rack areas. It lists natural and built terrain features including gradient, tree spacings and mound drains; species and productivity; stand development; and future management intentions as factors. Wider spacing works better on flat, readily worked terrain than steep slopes where harvester movement is more restricted.

**Numbers and conditions — not universal rules:** in standard Irish conifer planting layouts, conventional forestry often installs racks in **1 in 7 rows**, with **6 inter-rack rows**. In early transformation practitioners *might* widen to **1 in 8 or 9 rows or greater**, with **16–20 m between racks**, if conditions allow. For even-aged broadleaf or diverse conifer sites with potentially high-value timber, the page discusses **1 in 9 or 1 in 10** spacing, approximately **20 m**; the edge of this sentence is partially obscured by the page curve, so the exact parenthetical should be audited on the original. It also gives a specific mixed Scots pine/oak plantation example involving removal of **two out of three Scots pine rows** at first thinning, not a general rule. In developed CCF the text says **30 m or greater** might be viable when large valuable trees can be chainsaw-felled toward racks to within harvester reach. It describes possible rack abandonment as access into the inter-rack area improves after earlier thinnings. The book expressly says there is no hard-and-fast rack-spacing rule beyond balancing workable access and minimum disturbance.

**Reconciliation:** the old notes correctly identify 1-in-7, 1-in-8/9 and 16–20 m as context-dependent, but omit the 6 inter-rack rows, 1-in-9/10 broadleaf/diverse-conifer case, mixed Scots pine/oak example, and the explicit no-fixed-rule statement. The source has priority over the abbreviated notes. Images: `P040-IMG-01`, `P040-IMG-02`.

### Page 41 — Rack width, layout and orientation

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006215946.jpg`. Racks should be wide enough to avoid damage to adjacent trees; terrain influences width. Removing **one row** may suffice when following original planting lines on level ground. Cross-racking perpendicular to planted lines, or working on slope, may require removal of **more than one row** to allow machinery to move and work safely without damage. These are conditional descriptions, not a fixed metric rack width.

The book says rack distances should be as consistent as possible, with location adapted to soil, biodiversity, built features and slope. For conventional harvesting machinery, racks generally must run directly up and down slopes for safety and to minimise damage, since machinery travelling across a side slope can slide into downhill trees. It discusses hydrological advantages of working on contour but says present machinery and economics mostly dictate perpendicular-to-contour work; brash mats and dry-weather timing mitigate damage and water flows. Cross-racks sidling across slopes using natural or artificial terraces may be possible where terrain and budget permit. Image: `P041-IMG-01`.

**Reconciliation:** expands prior notes with row-count conditions, the stated reasons for slope direction, brash/dry-weather mitigation and conditional terraced cross-racks. No machine reach in metres or fixed rack width is given here.

### Page 42 — Headlands and rack ecology

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006215956.jpg`. Headlands connect rack exit points to forest roads. The source says headlands and linking tracks between stands receive the greatest machine traffic of unpaved infrastructure, and should be positioned on the driest, best ground; keep these connecting routes as short as possible to avoid heavy rutting. The rack-ecology box argues that post-harvest brightly lit racks can promote understorey growth and associated fauna; habitat changes as brash decomposes and canopy closes, with another felling starting a new sequence. These are the book's ecological claims, not a universal quantified outcome. The old notes omit the instruction to keep headland and linking routes short. Image: `P042-IMG-01`.

### Page 43 — CCF felling operational guidelines

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006220000.jpg`. The marking team communicates trees to remove and high-value trees to protect; machine operators must reassess changing conditions and biodiversity features potentially missed by markers. A developed understorey can limit visibility and a machine operator may see features from a better vantage point. Poor visibility also has health and safety implications; operators balance marking decisions, machine limits, valued trees and forest ecology, adhering to health and safety best practice. The old notes are consistent. Images: `P043-IMG-01` and `P043-IMG-02`.

### Page 44 — Guidelines for CCF harvester operators

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006220010.jpg`. Follow marking directives. Where felling would damage valued trees, leave and ring-bark the marked tree or remove an unmarked poor-quality tree to facilitate safe felling. Leave brash, avoid whole-tree harvesting and stump removal, and avoid damage to retained stems, crowns and roots. The page discusses shallow, outward-spreading roots and says that, exceptionally, racks can and should divert around trees of very high ecological value such as centuries-old native broadleaves. Monitor wet-weather ground conditions. If understorey shrubs impede access, cut cleanly rather than uproot to allow coppice regrowth; where multi-stemmed understorey is likely to obstruct machinery, chainsaw operators can coppice in advance.

The page distinguishes unmarked windthrow that is recently blown or still living and not in contact with the ground from fallen trees in contact with the ground; leave the latter for habitat and fertility unless very recently fallen, still carrying a living crown and of significant economic value. Read this exact wording carefully before applying it: the old notes' shorter statement omits the ground-contact qualification and the exceptional case. If ecological status of a marked tree changes, leave it standing when in doubt and select another poor-quality tree to release a valued tree. The book calls for ongoing owner–forester–contractor observation and communication. Background image: `P044-IMG-01`.

### Page 46 — Light forest operations, quality and species mix

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006220038.jpg`. The page distinguishes light operations outside harvest and commercial thinnings (the latter financially break even or profit). It describes early identification and release of quality, management of species mix/diversity and quality improvement. During early transformation activities may include non-commercial thinning (“tending”), shaping and pruning; in developed CCF these take place in the understorey community. Light regime, species mix and developmental stage affect their nature, frequency and intensity.

The book describes selecting vigorous, well-formed quality trees in regeneration patches and favouring desired or underrepresented species when releasing them. Its example: beech and oak seedlings regenerating after several thinnings of an even-aged Sitka stand in transformation may be favoured by disabling or removing some of the more numerous Sitka seedlings. This is an example for diversity, not a measured productivity benefit or fixed species quota. The prior notes' account is supported. Images: `P046-IMG-01–02`.

### Page 47 (number inferred) — Protection and early-stage release

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006220040.jpg`. While disabling direct competitors of quality trees, retain the wider understorey for biodiversity, protection, education, qualification and improvement. The text describes noncompeting trees and shrubs as potential physical barriers against deer browsing and fraying, and as encouraging vertical growth and suppressing side branches and epicormic growth. Young competitors may be snapped by bending the upper section until it breaks; the damaged tree remains alive, retaining educational/barrier functions. Larger competitors may be dealt with using a small saw, billhook or slash hook. The page describes mini-thinning or halo-thinning depending on the quality species' light demand, and ring-barking/girdling for larger saplings and early poles. The prior notes are consistent; the page number remains inferred because its footer is not visible. Images: `P047-IMG-01–02`.

### Page 48 — Later-stage release and light checks

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006220050.jpg`. For poles, use halo thinning or re-spacing depending on species and earlier intervention. Halo thinning is more often used for light-demanding species with heavy competition; re-spacing does not remove every surrounding tree and **perhaps one or two** main competitors are felled. In this Irish CCF context the book describes re-spacing as non-commercial thinning at sapling or pole stage and notes it can involve cutting back or singling poles from coppiced hardwood stumps. If earlier seedling/sapling release occurred, later non-commercial pole thinning may be unnecessary depending on species mix and light demand; a later combined CCF felling might suffice.

Timing and intensity depend on monitoring and light requirements: oak is an example for earlier heavier intervention; western red cedar may wait longer for lower-intensity intervention. The book recommends early snapping or slashing following felling to benefit from increased diffuse light. Its quick stem-light check compares apical shoot growth with lateral shoot growth at the crown top: in good light the apical growth is longer, and in poor light the reverse. The old notes capture the core but omit coppice singling and the conditional possibility of omitting later pole thinning. Image: `P048-IMG-01`.

### Page 49 — Competing vegetation and quality improvement

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006220057.jpg`. The book describes brambles as ecologically valuable, but recommends localised slashing when unwanted vegetation grows higher than quality stems in natural regeneration. It prefers natural lateral suppression by competition for tree form, while early transformation may require shaping or pruning where a multi-layered understorey has yet to develop. It says standard shaping/pruning best practice should be applied, and the owner-oriented box advises consulting foresters on mix, timing, intensity and techniques. The old notes are consistent; the page additionally states formative shaping is a requirement when establishing CCF in afforestation/reforestation. Image: `P049-IMG-01`.

### Page 50 — Species enrichment

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006220112.jpg`. Regeneration reflects local or nearby seed sources, so enrichment can introduce desired absent species. The page suggests selecting site-matched native or proven non-invasive exotic/naturalised species and considering soil, compatibility, aims, light and browsers; high browser populations may require shelters or exclosures for broadleaves and some palatable conifers. Shade-tolerant species are discussed as useful earlier in transformation under lower light; compatibility also depends on growth rate and maturity. Prior notes are broadly consistent; glare/curvature obscures portions of the light discussion. Image: `P050-IMG-01`.

### Page 51 — Targeted enrichment planting

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006220115.jpg`. Concentrate enrichment where success is most likely. Felling or natural-disturbance gaps can accommodate light-demanding species such as oak, with numbers and spacing promoting straight growth. The text says to put the **more light-demanding species at the northern edges of gaps**. It then separately says shade-tolerant species such as beech and western red cedar can be placed in diffuse light. It generally favours fewer well-protected trees in groups over extensive intimate mixing, and says enrichment should not take place on permanent rack networks. Images: `P051-IMG-01–02`.

**Reconciliation correction:** the old notes assign northern gap edges to the shade-tolerant species. The page assigns those edges to the more **light-demanding** species. The source wins; no further causal explanation is assumed.

### Page 53 — Creating CCF forests

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006222619.jpg`. The book presents three routes: transformation; afforestation or reforestation; natural succession (rewilding). It characterises natural succession as often too slow for landowner aims and, under high deer populations, unachievable without exclusion or sustained culling. It describes pioneer shrubs and trees eventually succeeded by high forest and says the resulting forest can be managed using other CCF principles. ### Page 54 — Transformation

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006222651.jpg`. Transformation is described as changing an even-aged plantation into an irregular high forest; in Ireland this often involves even-aged conifers of one or limited species. Initiation is the first few dedicated CCF thinnings. The book points to legacy forest conditions, humic soil, microclimate and some biodiversity as potential starting assets. It also describes transforming other even-aged or mixed stands, while older mixed woodland already exhibiting desired CCF features may instead begin CCF management directly. Some right-column line endings are curved and require source audit. Prior notes' qualification against claiming species diversity is unnecessary remains appropriate. Image: `P054-IMG-01`.

### Page 55 (number inferred) — Candidates for transformation

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006222701.jpg`. Suitability and difficulty depend on species, development stage, past management and site. The page favours early intervention in a stand with some diversity and limited previous thinning, with first or second thinning described as a prime moment; high initial stocking and nearby seed sources provide options. Stability is central: it describes older neglected, tall/slender stands with receded crowns or unstable/exposed sites as unsuitable in the stated circumstances, and notes clear-fell and replanting as a possible alternative with loss of forest conditions for a period. Early GDT may be considered for young stands in exposed/unstable conditions. The old notes are consistent; printed number remains inferred. Image: `P055-IMG-01`.

### Page 56 — Stages in transformation

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006222755.jpg`. Four illustrated stages: (1) initiating transformation by first and subsequent thinnings for quality, stability and long-term harvest infrastructure; (2) continued transformation, improving quality and managing light for regeneration; (3) late-stage transformation, promoting structural irregularity and social structure including waiting room, sprinters and high canopy; (4) maintaining a CCF forest through regular harvesting of growth increment while retaining a resilient ecosystem. It says selective felling and assessment apply throughout, with extra initiating-phase considerations. The stages are the source's model; the drawing does not fix a timescale. Prior notes agree. Image sequence: `P056-IMG-01`.

### Page 57 — Selective felling in transformation

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006222806.jpg`. The boxed guidance cautions against removing small trees merely to tidy or low-thin; targets large poorly formed “wolf” trees to release quality/diversity and make regeneration light, while possibly retaining large poor-quality trees for structure/stability where high-quality large trees are scarce. Some wolves can be ring-barked for standing deadwood. In older even-aged stands it suggests light but more frequent thinning for stability. A **bio group** is explicitly distinguished from a group of bio trees: two or more close-grown trees with shared rooting and crown behaviour, to be managed as one and retained for stability. It favours deep crowns of approximately **one-third to one-half of tree height**, describes standing/fallen deadwood and natural-disturbance openings, and says broadleaves and deciduous conifers such as larch need earlier, stronger release than conifers. It cautions that later felling opportunities exist, balancing heavy marking with stability. The old notes are broadly consistent; the bio-group distinction is made explicit here. Image: `P057-IMG-01`.

### Page 58 — Graduated density thinning (GDT)

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006222832.jpg`. The book describes GDT as developed in Wales to preserve stand stability in Sitka spruce plantations transforming to CCF, with limited use and an Irish efficacy trial mentioned. At first intervention **every eighth row** is removed as a rack; in the inter-rack area remove **40%** from the first row adjacent to the rack, **20%** in the second and **10%** in the third. The **fourth row**, halfway between racks, remains untouched for stability. Removal selections reflect poor quality or proximity to quality trees. At second thinning, initial racks are abandoned and the intact fourth row becomes the new rack, with the same pattern repeated.

At third thinning permanent racks are chosen; whether the same thinning pattern is repeated depends on stand development. **Once managers are confident of stand stability**, the GDT pattern can be abandoned and selective felling is generally used. The page presents this as a specific method, not a general prescription for every CCF stand. Diagram: `P058-IMG-01`.

**Reconciliation correction:** the old notes say that by third thinning racks are permanent and selective felling takes over. This overstates the timing: the page conditions abandonment of the GDT pattern on confidence in stability and allows a decision to repeat it at the third thinning.

### Page 59 — Shelterwood systems

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006222843.jpg`. The book describes using an existing even-aged stand for shelter and seed, thinning to enable regeneration and continuing harvest as the new cohort develops. It lists uniform, strip, group and irregular shelterwood. This is an option in transformation when a stand is advanced and trees large but stable, and is described as especially suited to light-demanding species **where competing vegetation will not be an issue**. Uniform shelterwood eventually removes the last original cohort when the replacement establishes; the book suggests it may be useful in very high-yield-class Sitka stands. Strip shelterwood uses successive strips over years for shelter, reduced windthrow risk and less regeneration damage. The old notes omit the competing-vegetation condition. Image: `P059-IMG-01`.

### Page 60 — Shelterwood and time to transform

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006222901.jpg`. Group shelterwood expands felling from existing regeneration or disturbance over subsequent years. Irregular shelterwood varies regeneration felling in space and time and retains some of the original cohort longer, possibly without removing all at once. The page describes retaining forest conditions/canopy and using natural regeneration for restocking as shared CCF principles in four shelterwood variants. It discusses adaptations and says that, when shelterwood is used for transformation and regeneration reaches sapling/pole stage, selective felling will usually become the main maintenance method.

**Numerical context:** for a Sitka spruce plantation where transformation began at the **second thinning**, the book says it **might take 40 years** to achieve transformation; this is a conditional example, not a universal duration. The adjacent-page photograph `IMG20261006222904.jpg` shows the clipped portion: it says the earliest mixed-conifer Irish sites have been managed under CCF for around **25 years** and reached late-stage transformation. This is an example of particular sites, not a universal duration. Image: `P060-IMG-01`.

### Page 75 — Basal area rules of thumb and marking

**Primary source:** `IMG20261006223057.jpg`. Site productivity and species light preferences affect target basal area. A very productive site with shade-tolerant species **may support over 30 m²/ha**, whereas a site with very light-demanding species **might require less than 20 m²/ha** for regeneration; both are conditional illustrations. General advice is to remove at most **20% of pre-harvest basal area** and **no more than the stand increment**; the source says at least **two inventories** are needed to calculate the latter. Basal area should be measured before harvest and its reduction treated as a stand/site average, with varying local marking intensity. The page warns that too much basal area can limit lower-canopy light and regeneration, while too little can favour competing vegetation; it is less representative in advanced, spatially variable stands and does not describe distribution.

The marking box describes measuring pre-harvest sample plots, marking in and around them, measuring the marked trees' combined basal area and comparing its reduction against the target to adjust marking intensity. **Reconciliation:** the older notes record the 20% advice but omit the separate “no more than stand increment” constraint and its two-inventory qualification. No diagram or photo is present on this page; the text box is guidance, not a visual evidence image.

## Source overview and terminology — synthesis of available pages

The photographed portion presents CCF as recurring selective management that protects forest conditions, regeneration, stability, biodiversity and timber quality while making felling and extraction practicable. It describes transformation of even-aged plantations, new establishment and natural succession as different creation routes (pp. 53–56, 61). Operations connect assessment and tree marking to harvesting, permanent access, protection of retained features, light management, regeneration and monitoring; this sequence is a synthesis of the reviewed pages, not a verbatim prescription (pp. 37–44, 46–51, 56–58, 69, 74–77, 84–89). ## Topic synthesis — available photographed pages

- **Access and racks (pp. 39–42, 58, 76, 85, 87; P039–P042, P058, P085, P087):** permanent networks support machine access and constrain disturbance, with headlands linking to roads. Spacing is conditional on stand and terrain; the GDT row pattern is a special method. The marks for proposed rack-tree removal and retained route edges have distinct forms. No entrance-only rule or universal rack spacing is established in these reviewed pages.
- **Transformation and selection (pp. 53–60; P053–P060):** the book describes four stages, stability assessment, selection of quality and retention of structure, and alternative GDT/shelterwood methods. Examples and conditional advice remain site-specific.
- **Release, regeneration and enrichment (pp. 46–51, 60, 66–68; P046–P051, P060, P066–P068):** identify quality, release specific competitors, retain useful understorey, assess light and match planting species and groups to site and seed availability. ## E. Tree marking / spray-paint guidance

Page 84 presents a system developed by Pro Silva Ireland for Irish CCF thinning and states it has been widely adopted in private and state forests for consistency across harvest sites. That is the source's claim; independent extent of adoption is not assessed. It groups removal, ring barking, fallen deadwood and high-cut standing deadwood under a bright “hot” colour, usually orange or pink; retained trees use a “cool” colour, white or blue. The category terms do not make all marks interchangeable.

- **Selected removal (p. 84):** coloured diagonal slashes around the tree, visible from all angles, at approximately 2 m height. Selection examples: poor quality, favouring better-quality trees, market readiness, releasing regeneration, favouring structural or species diversity, and promoting stability.
- **Removal for harvesting/extraction (p. 85):** coloured vertical stripes on four sides; rack marks show the proposed machine path's direction. Generally used in first thinning, with possible later use for extending or adding racks. The text does not state stripe height or require each rack tree to be individually marked.
- **Ring barking (p. 85):** coloured zig-zag identifies standing deadwood to be created by ring barking. Example situations include machinery unable to reach a tree when cutting a route to one tree is not cost effective; risk of damaging important surrounding trees; or insufficient standing deadwood, generally using poor timber-quality trees. The page says not close to roads or public walking paths.
- **Fallen deadwood (p. 85):** a round symbol bisected by a horizontal line means fell and leave on the ground. Examples include insufficient fallen deadwood, generally using poor-quality, undesired or overrepresented species; and a windthrown tree interfering with a retained tree while already developing microhabitat indicators. The latter is left to provide habitat as it decomposes.
- **Standing deadwood (p. 86):** a round symbol bisected by a vertical line means high cut to create safe standing deadwood. Example conditions include insufficient standing deadwood and a harvester able to fell at 4 m height, leaving a standing stump. The source says it can be used near paths in its stated circumstances, primarily on conifers that will not pollard, and also on broadleaves where pollarding is useful. Examples include vertical structural diversity, suppressing side branching on neighbouring quality trees, and interim support for a vulnerable broadleaf until its stem circumference increases.
- **Biodiversity retention (p. 86):** white or blue triangle on 3 or 4 sides depending on tree size, visible from all sides. A tree scribe may replace spray paint where paint is inappropriate. The tree must not be damaged in other operations; not all biodiversity-feature trees need marking, only those of very high biodiversity value. Examples include veteran trees of diverse species, standing deadwood habitat trees, trees with nests or dreys of important species, and underrepresented tree species.
- **High timber-quality retention (p. 87):** white or blue ring around the circumference at approximately 2 m, visible at a distance; the tree must not be damaged during operations. Quality trees are generally identified early—first thinning for broadleaves and second for conifers—and favoured in successive thinnings, but may be selected at any stage. Touch up the ring at each subsequent marking; in visually or ecologically sensitive areas, temporary ribbon can be used for the harvest instead of a permanent paint ring.
- **Permanent rack/headland route retention (p. 87):** two parallel horizontal lines on retained trees either side of permanent racks, on the side visible from the rack or headland entrance as machinery travels along the route. The text later identifies the existing mark as white or blue and describes overpainting it orange or pink and re-marking for removal if necessary for silvicultural reasons. Specific mark height, distance between marked trees and a requirement to mark only entrance trees are not stated.
