## B. Executive summary

- **Purpose and access:** Trees selected for removal to accommodate harvesting and extraction carry rack marks indicating the direction of a proposed machine path or rack (p. 85). Trees retained beside permanent racks can demarcate rack or headland routes for machinery (p. 87). The pages do not offer a fuller account of why racks are used.
- **Layout and spacing:** A proposed path has a direction and permanent routes can be marked in the stand, but no rack spacing, width, geometry, planting-row relation or numerical machine reach is given. **NOT ESTABLISHED BY THIS SOURCE** (pp. 84–87).
- **Establishment and reuse:** Rack removal marks are generally used in first thinning, but may be used in later thinnings to extend racks or create additional ones (p. 85). Permanent route marks are useful where old routes are not immediately obvious or excess racking requires some rack abandonment (p. 87). A repeated-use route is implied by “permanent”; a maintenance or reuse schedule is not stated.
- **Physical marking:** Coloured vertical stripes on four sides identify trees for removal for the proposed route (p. 85). A separate mark—two parallel horizontal lines on retained trees either side of permanent racks—demarcates routes and is placed on the side visible from the entrance as machinery travels along the route (p. 87). Neither passage says only the first/entrance trees are marked, or that every tree in a route receives a mark.
- **Colour and CCF:** The source presents a Pro Silva Ireland standard for CCF thinning, with a bright “hot” colour, usually orange or pink, for trees to be removed or treated for deadwood, and a “cool” colour, white or blue, for retained trees (p. 84). Shapes and purposes are distinct. The pages do not specify how rack establishment in a plantation conversion differs from conventional first thinning.

## E. Tree marking / spray-paint guidance

Page 84 presents a system developed by Pro Silva Ireland for Irish CCF thinning and states it has been widely adopted in private and state forests for consistency across harvest sites. That is the source's claim; independent extent of adoption is not assessed. It groups removal, ring barking, fallen deadwood and high-cut standing deadwood under a bright “hot” colour, usually orange or pink; retained trees use a “cool” colour, white or blue. The category terms do not make all marks interchangeable.

- **Selected removal (p. 84):** coloured diagonal slashes around the tree, visible from all angles, at approximately 2 m height. Selection examples: poor quality, favouring better-quality trees, market readiness, releasing regeneration, favouring structural or species diversity, and promoting stability.
- **Removal for harvesting/extraction (p. 85):** coloured vertical stripes on four sides; rack marks show the proposed machine path's direction. Generally used in first thinning, with possible later use for extending or adding racks. The text does not state stripe height or require each rack tree to be individually marked.
- **Ring barking (p. 85):** coloured zig-zag identifies standing deadwood to be created by ring barking. Example situations include machinery unable to reach a tree when cutting a route to one tree is not cost effective; risk of damaging important surrounding trees; or insufficient standing deadwood, generally using poor timber-quality trees. The page says not close to roads or public walking paths.
- **Fallen deadwood (p. 85):** a round symbol bisected by a horizontal line means fell and leave on the ground. Examples include insufficient fallen deadwood, generally using poor-quality, undesired or overrepresented species; and a windthrown tree interfering with a retained tree while already developing microhabitat indicators. The latter is left to provide habitat as it decomposes.
- **Standing deadwood (p. 86):** a round symbol bisected by a vertical line means high cut to create safe standing deadwood. Example conditions include insufficient standing deadwood and a harvester able to fell at 4 m height, leaving a standing stump. The source says it can be used near paths in its stated circumstances, primarily on conifers that will not pollard, and also on broadleaves where pollarding is useful. Examples include vertical structural diversity, suppressing side branching on neighbouring quality trees, and interim support for a vulnerable broadleaf until its stem circumference increases.
- **Biodiversity retention (p. 86):** white or blue triangle on 3 or 4 sides depending on tree size, visible from all sides. A tree scribe may replace spray paint where paint is inappropriate. The tree must not be damaged in other operations; not all biodiversity-feature trees need marking, only those of very high biodiversity value. Examples include veteran trees of diverse species, standing deadwood habitat trees, trees with nests or dreys of important species, and underrepresented tree species.
- **High timber-quality retention (p. 87):** white or blue ring around the circumference at approximately 2 m, visible at a distance; the tree must not be damaged during operations. Quality trees are generally identified early—first thinning for broadleaves and second for conifers—and favoured in successive thinnings, but may be selected at any stage. Touch up the ring at each subsequent marking; in visually or ecologically sensitive areas, temporary ribbon can be used for the harvest instead of a permanent paint ring.
- **Permanent rack/headland route retention (p. 87):** two parallel horizontal lines on retained trees either side of permanent racks, on the side visible from the rack or headland entrance as machinery travels along the route. The text later identifies the existing mark as white or blue and describes overpainting it orange or pink and re-marking for removal if necessary for silvicultural reasons. Specific mark height, distance between marked trees and a requirement to mark only entrance trees are not stated.
